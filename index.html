A visual walkthrough

How a machine learns to sort things

From one straight fence to XOR — built entirely from ideas you can watch move. Every section has a picture you can play with.

Underneath all of this sits one small idea. A machine sorts things into two groups by drawing a boundary, and classifies anything new by checking which side of the boundary it falls on. Everything below is just different boundaries.

1.One straight fence

Picture each thing you want to sort as a point on a grid — one of the little circles — placed by its measurements. A single unit, a perceptron, draws one straight fence. Everything on one side it calls yellow; the other side, cyan.

The fence has a violet arrow that shows which way it faces, and the fence always sits crosswise (at a right angle) to that arrow. What controls the arrow is two numbers — one for X, one for Y — called the weights (written w). Changing the weights re-aims the arrow, and the fence swings to match: weights → aim the arrow → place the fence. You never move the fence by hand. The fence is aimed right once every yellow point sits on the side the arrow faces, with every cyan point on the other side.

To check one point, the machine asks: is this point on the side the arrow points to? A point's two bracket numbers are its X (first) and Y (second). It compares the arrow and the point in X, and in Y, sees whether they go the same way or opposite ways, and tallies it into one number — a plus means "yes, arrow's side," a minus means "no, the other side." (When the arrow points straight up/down this is just "check the Y number.")

The machine learns by fixing mistakes. When a point is on the wrong side, the rule is simple: take the old weights and add the wrong point's two numbers to get the new weights — swinging the arrow toward a yellow point (or subtract, to swing away from a cyan one). Nothing is multiplied; you just add or subtract the point. (Shorthand: w_new = w_old + y·x, where "y·x" only means "the point, flipped to a minus if it's cyan.") Each tap first plans the point to fix and checks it; the next tap nudges the arrow so you watch it swing.

2.When one fence isn't enough

One fence only gives you "this side vs. that side." But suppose the group you want sits in the middle, with the other group both above and below it. No single straight line can carve out a middle — wherever you place it, the outer group lands on both sides. The fix: use two fences and keep what's between them. Here it is doing real work.

3.A real example: body temperature

Now the up–down axis is a real measurement: body temperature. Too cold is bad; too hot is bad; healthy is the band between. A single rule like "higher = sicker" would be wrong, because both extremes are bad. You need two fences — a low and a high threshold — and "healthy if between them."

This is how you classify something new. Drag the slider to set a patient's temperature and watch the verdict. Nothing is re-learned — the fences are already placed; you just check which side the new reading falls on.

4.XOR is the lane, tilted 45°

XOR is a famous pattern one fence can't solve. Its four cases sit in opposite corners: yellow top-left and bottom-right, cyan top-right and bottom-left. Any single line ends up with one of each colour on each side. But the two yellow points line up along a diagonal — so it's the same lane trick, tilted 45°.

5.Building a layered perceptron

To fence off a middle band you need two fences and a way to combine them. Here's exactly how three little perceptrons do it: two of them (a hidden layer) each draw one fence and answer yes/no, and a third (the output) combines their answers. Each fence is its own perceptron — same idea as section 1; the new part is the combine.

Note: below, the two fences are placed by hand so we can focus on the combine. A real network would learn all three at once, using the same nudging you saw in section 1, with the output's mistakes passed back to steer each fence (that's "backpropagation").

6.How this scales to the real world

Real problems add more features and more fences. With hundreds of measurements the "grid" becomes a space too big to picture, and the network draws many fences that combine into complicated boundaries. But the machinery is exactly what you watched: a thing becomes a point from its measurements → neurons draw fences → a later layer combines them into a shape a single line couldn't make → a new thing is classified by which region it lands in.
